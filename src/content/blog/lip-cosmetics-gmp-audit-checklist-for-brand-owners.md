## What a Brand Owner Should Check Before Approving a Lip Factory

ISO 22716 covers cosmetic good manufacturing practice at the plant level. GMPC certification adds another recognized manufacturing practice framework, but a certificate alone does not prove that a particular 6 ml gloss was filled correctly. Beautychain can arrange an ISO 22716 and GMPC dual-certification factory audit, with the certificates available for verification during a qualified commercial review. The important point is traceability from the certificate to the production record.

A buyer should ask to see how raw materials are received under lot control at 25 °C, where approved formulas are locked, and how a deviation is closed. If the answer is only “our quality team checks it,” the audit has not reached the operating detail. A usable record should identify the supplier lot, weighing operator, scale ID, processing time, fill temperature, and final release decision.

For water-containing lip formulas, the preservative system needs more than a supplier statement. ISO 11930 challenge testing applies to formulas containing water, while an anhydrous gloss can often avoid that specific test because there is no water phase to support microbial growth. It is one reason some brands launch an anhydrous product first, then move to a lip mask or water-based tint after the documentation process is familiar.

A factory audit should also separate plant certification from product compliance. ISO 22716 is not a product registration, and it does not replace a CPSR. Under EU Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, the CPSR Part A is the formula safety assessment and Part B is the plant GMP statement. Part A alone will not place a cosmetic product on the EU market.

The EU Responsible Person files CPNP notification before the product is placed on the market. The CPNP portal itself is free, while the commercial cost normally sits in the CPSR, often around EUR 400-900 per formula. For the United States, MoCRA requires facility registration, product listing, and a responsible person named on the label. The United States does not use the same file structure as the EU.

A lip cosmetics GMP audit checklist for brand owners should therefore ask for a document index before sampling begins. MSDS, COA, and test reports are provided on qualified commercial inquiry, with the exact file set depending on the formula, destination market, packaging, and claim language. A buyer should not accept a generic document folder containing files for another shade or another component.

The fill-weight control is a good example. On a declared 6 ml gloss, the working tolerance may be plus or minus 3%, but the lower bound must still support the declared net content. A line check that averages 6.02 ml does not resolve a unit that falls below the declared minimum. The audit should show the sampling frequency, balance calibration, operator sign-off, and action taken when a result moves outside the internal limit. Shade approval needs the same discipline. A practical approval target is Delta E less than or equal to 0.5 under D65 lighting, while the accepted difference between production batches may widen to Delta E less than or equal to 1.0. Pearlescent and high-tint systems can read two shades deeper after hot filling, so a bench drawdown is not enough. The retained sample should be compared after the actual fill process.

Rheology belongs in the audit because applicator performance is a production issue, not only a marketing preference. A wand gloss usually runs well around 1,200-2,500 mPa·s at 25 °C. Below 1,200 mPa·s, the formula can run off the applicator; above 2,500 mPa·s, it may stop flowing back into the tube. A doe-foot generally gives about 300 mPa·s more headroom than a brush.

An anhydrous gloss filled at 70-75 °C needs a pre-warmed tube. If the component is cold, the first 200 units can skin over at the nozzle and create inconsistent appearance or drag. The audit question is simple: what is the approved fill temperature, how is it recorded, and how does the line operator handle the first-off units?

Pigment dispersion should also be checked after time, not only immediately after mixing. An under-milled anhydrous base can settle over 10-14 days and produce a visible gradient in the tube. A factory that releases the sample after 24 hours may miss the failure. The buyer should request the dispersion method, hold time, retained sample plan, and the criteria used for visual acceptance.

Flavour compatibility can create another delayed problem. Vanilla or peppermint oils may plasticise some TPE applicators over 8-10 weeks, leaving a sticky tip in the stability pull. That failure often does not appear in the bench sample. The packaging audit should link the applicator material, flavour load, formula lot, and stability observation.

Packaging compatibility is not a catalogue exercise. A 5 ml or 6 ml round PETG tube may suit a clear gloss, while a 4.5 ml slim tube can better support a narrow premium silhouette. An 8 ml squeeze tube with slant tip changes the dispensing experience, and a 3.5 ml ABS stick requires a different filling and cooling sequence. The correct choice depends on the formula and line equipment. PCR PETG is available at 30% and 50%, but clarity visibly drops at 50%. A refillable aluminium shell with a PP inner cup keeps aluminium away from the formula, which matters for compatibility and filling control. Bamboo-clad ABS caps carry a separate 4-5 week supplier lead time and are the most common packaging-related cause of a slipped launch date in our experience.

For environmental claims, COSMOS and ECOCERT assess the formula and raw-material chain, including a minimum of 95% ingredients of natural origin under the relevant standard and its processing restrictions. An ISO certificate for the plant is not an eco certificate. “Organic” is not a regulated claim under EU Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, so legal label wording and a COSMOS audit remain separate tests.

Testing should be planned against the destination market and formula design. A common accelerated program uses 12 weeks at 45 °C plus 3 freeze-thaw cycles between -10 °C and 25 °C, with 24 hours at each temperature. Thermal shock can use 24 hours at 50 °C followed by 24 hours at -5 °C for 3 cycles, while the team watches for leakage, separation, cap failure, and label lift.

Each batch should have heavy-metal testing for Pb, Cd, Hg, and As, together with microbial checks for TPC, yeast and mould, and absence of S. aureus and P. aeruginosa where applicable. These tests do not turn a cosmetic into a medical product. They support safe quality release and help a buyer defend a documented specification.

Sampling is the point where a brand owner can test the factory without committing to a full retail run. A custom brief can usually produce samples within 7 working days after approval, or within 3 days from an existing colour card. A pilot batch starts at 500 pieces per shade, which is a filling MOQ rather than a packaging MOQ; stock components make that run practical because new packaging often starts at 3,000-5,000 units.

The sample fee is refundable against the order. That sequence reduces the risk of buying 5,000 caps for a formula that still fails an 8-week applicator check.

The distinction between private label and ODM should be recorded in the audit scope. Private label usually means a proven base with controlled shade and packaging changes. Full custom ODM can alter the texture, pigment load, flavour, applicator, fill method, and compliance file, but each change can add development time. A brand owner should also inspect corrective-action history. One late cap, one failed microbial result, or one batch with a fill average of 5.96 ml does not automatically disqualify a factory. The useful question is whether the deviation was investigated within a defined period, whether affected lots were contained, and whether the preventive action was verified on the next production run.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What should a lip cosmetics GMP audit checklist for brand owners include first?

Start with the formula revision, material-lot control, scale calibration, batch record, and fill-weight checks for the intended 6 ml or 8 ml pack. Then confirm the plant certificate, responsible quality person, release criteria, and deviation process. A certificate dated 2026 has limited value if the factory cannot connect it to the batch record for the buyer’s shade.

### Q: Is ISO 22716 enough for an EU lip gloss launch?

No. ISO 22716 addresses plant GMP, while an EU launch also needs a CPSR, an EU Responsible Person, CPNP notification, compliant artwork, and supporting product records. Part A of the CPSR is the safety assessment, and Part B records the GMP statement. The CPNP filing is free at the portal, but the CPSR commonly costs EUR 400-900 per formula.

### Q: What does a 500-piece lip gloss pilot actually cover?

A 500-piece pilot is a filling MOQ per shade, not a new-packaging MOQ. It can validate a 6 ml formula, shade under D65, fill weight within plus or minus 3%, applicator behaviour, and first-off appearance. Components usually start at 3,000-5,000 units, so the 500-piece run normally uses stock packaging rather than a newly tooled component.

### Q: How can a buyer reduce shade disputes between approval and production?

Approve the shade against a physical drawdown and a hot-filled sample, using D65 and a documented Delta E target of less than or equal to 0.5. Production batches may use a wider Delta E limit of less than or equal to 1.0. High-tint systems can read two shades deeper after filling at 70-75 °C, so a screen image or cold bench sample should not be the only reference.

### Q: Which files should a qualified lip cosmetics buyer request?

Ask for the applicable MSDS, COA, stability results, microbial results, heavy-metal results, packaging compatibility records, and formula information needed for the destination market. For EU products, request CPSR inputs and CPNP coordination details; for US products, confirm the MoCRA facility, product listing, and responsible-person arrangements.
